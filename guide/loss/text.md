## 1. From a network to an optimization problem

This chapter follows Sections II-A and II-B of the survey. It builds the two objects every later chapter uses: the loss, and its local shape, described by the gradient and the Hessian.

Start with data. A training set is a list of $n$ examples $\{(x_i, y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$: inputs $x_i$ (an image, a sentence, a vector of numbers) and the outputs $y_i$ we want the network to produce. A network $f(x;\theta)$ turns an input $x$ into a prediction. Its behavior is set by the [[parameters]] $\theta$, all its weights and biases stacked into one long vector $\theta \in \mathbb{R}^d$.

To say how wrong a prediction is, pick a **per-example loss** $\phi$: a number that is zero when the prediction equals the target and grows as they differ. Two common choices are the squared error $\phi(\hat y, y) = (\hat y - y)^2$ and the cross-entropy for classification. Training minimizes the average over the training set, the **empirical risk**:

$$L(\theta) = \frac1n \sum_{i=1}^n \phi\big(f(x_i;\theta),\, y_i\big) = \frac1n\sum_{i=1}^n L_i(\theta).$$

$L_i(\theta)$ is the loss on example $i$ alone. Every later algorithm only ever sees $L$ through its values and slopes.

The smallest possible example is a straight line, $f(x; w, b) = wx + b$, with only two parameters: the slope $w$ and the intercept $b$. Every pair $(w, b)$ is one line, and $L(w, b)$ is the average squared error of that line on the data. The pairs form a plane, and the loss turns the plane into a surface: the [[landscape]].

## 2. Why the loss of a network cannot be convex

A function is **[[convex]]** if the straight segment between any two points of its graph lies on or above the graph. A convex loss has no bad valleys: every local minimum is global, and the set of minimizers is itself convex. In particular, if $\theta^\star$ and $\theta^{\star\star}$ are both minimizers, so is their average.

Neural networks break this through [[symmetry]]. Take a layer with two hidden units. Swap them: exchange their incoming weights, and exchange their outgoing weights. The network computes exactly the same function, so the loss does not change. Call the swapped parameters $\pi(\theta)$. If $\theta^\star$ is a minimizer, so is $\pi(\theta^\star)$.

Now suppose $L$ were convex. Then the average $\tfrac12(\theta^\star + \pi(\theta^\star))$ would also be a minimizer. But in that average the two hidden units have been blended into identical copies. The network has effectively lost a unit and computes a different, typically worse, function. So the average is not a minimizer, and $L$ is not convex.

The same symmetry produces many equivalent minima, one for each way of ordering the hidden units, connected through regions of higher loss. Those regions are natural places for saddle points to appear.

## 3. The gradient: which way is down

Gradient descent needs a direction. The **[[gradient]]** $\nabla L(\theta) \in \mathbb{R}^d$ collects the partial derivatives $\partial L / \partial \theta_j$: how fast the loss changes when only parameter $j$ moves. Three facts about it matter for everything that follows.

- It points in the direction of **steepest increase** of $L$. So $-\nabla L$ is the direction of steepest decrease.
- Its length $\lVert \nabla L(\theta) \rVert$ is that steepest rate of change. A small gradient means the landscape is nearly flat at $\theta$.
- It is perpendicular to the **contour** (level curve) of $L$ through $\theta$.

## 4. Taylor's theorem: the loss near a point

Up close, every smooth function looks simple. For a small displacement $\Delta$, Taylor's theorem gives the local model

$$L(\theta + \Delta) \approx L(\theta) + \nabla L(\theta)^\top \Delta + \tfrac12\, \Delta^\top \nabla^2 L(\theta)\, \Delta.$$

The first two terms are a flat plane tangent to the surface: the [[taylor|first-order Taylor approximation]]. They explain gradient descent in one line. Take $\Delta = -\eta \nabla L(\theta)$, a small step of size $\eta$ against the gradient. The first-order term becomes $-\eta \lVert \nabla L(\theta)\rVert^2$, which is negative, so a small enough step decreases the loss.

## 5. The Hessian: how the loss bends

The second-order term uses the **[[hessian|Hessian]]** $\nabla^2 L(\theta) \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}$, the matrix of second derivatives $\partial^2 L / \partial\theta_j \partial\theta_k$. It measures **curvature**: how the slope itself changes as we move.

The Hessian is symmetric, and a symmetric matrix has a special set of directions. Its [[eigen|eigenvectors]] $v_1, \dots, v_d$ are perpendicular to each other, and along each one the matrix acts as a simple stretch by a real number, the eigenvalue $\lambda_j$. Moving a distance $\epsilon$ along $v_j$, the quadratic term of the Taylor model equals $\tfrac12 \epsilon^2 \lambda_j$. So each eigenvalue is the curvature along its own direction:

- $\lambda_j > 0$: the loss curves **up** along $v_j$, like a cup.
- $\lambda_j < 0$: the loss curves **down** along $v_j$, like a cap. This is a direction of **negative curvature**.
- $\lambda_j = 0$: the loss is flat along $v_j$ to second order.

The smallest and largest eigenvalues get names, $\lambda_{\min}$ and $\lambda_{\max}$. At a point where the gradient vanishes, the first-order term of the Taylor model is zero. Negative curvature is then the only first- or second-order information that reveals a way downhill. This one sentence is the seed of the whole saddle literature (chapters 6 to 8). And $\lambda_{\max}$, the sharpest upward curvature, is the quantity the large-step literature is about (chapters 9 to 13).

**What to try.** Start from the default $H = \begin{bmatrix}4 & 2\\ 2 & 4\end{bmatrix}$, whose eigenvalues are $6$ and $2$ along the diagonals. Turn $u$ all the way round. Then set $\lambda_2$ negative to make a saddle, and set it to zero to make a flat direction.

**What to notice.** The curvature along $u$ always lies between the two eigenvalues: it is a weighted blend of them. With one positive and one negative eigenvalue, the surface is a saddle, and the red tube is the way down.
:::

:::key What to keep from this chapter
- The loss $L(\theta)$ is an average of per-example losses over $d$ parameters. Symmetries of the network make it non-convex, with many equivalent minima and saddles between them.
- The gradient gives the steepest direction of increase; $-\nabla L$ is the way down.
- The Hessian gives the curvature. Its eigenvalues are the curvatures along perpendicular directions: $\lambda < 0$ curves down, $\lambda > 0$ curves up.
:::
